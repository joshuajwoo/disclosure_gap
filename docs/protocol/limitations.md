# Interpretation and limitations

## What the portfolio can establish

The core project can demonstrate engineering behavior: versioned records validate correctly, temporal and privacy rules are enforced, raw-to-feature lineage is auditable, supported schema changes preserve meaning, and seeded synthetic scenarios produce expected pipeline behavior. These are claims about the software, not about human psychology.

Synthetic coefficients, charts, and apparent associations are generated test conditions. They are never evidence that an intention–action gap predicts social anxiety in real people. A successful recovery of a seeded effect validates a pipeline assumption; a null scenario that remains null is equally valuable. Any report must label the data source and keep synthetic validation separate from optional empirical results.

The case-study question is personally meaningful, but it is not claimed as a new theory or breakthrough. Social anxiety, avoidance, self-disclosure, safety behavior, and help-seeking already have substantial literatures; representative starting sources are listed in `PLAN.md`. The engineering contribution is the system built around that established domain.

## Meaning of privacy and disclosure

Lower disclosure is not inherently harmful. It may reflect healthy boundaries, cultural norms, privacy preference, lack of a safe relationship, concern about consequences, or the absence of a suitable opportunity. The primary feature is a behavioral self-report, not a symptom score, and the application must not score a person negatively for keeping something private.

## Limits of an optional real-data pilot

An observational pilot can estimate an association but cannot establish that an intention–action gap causes later social anxiety. Reverse causation, stable individual differences, time-varying stressors, perceived relationship safety, and measurement overlap can confound results. Baseline adjustment does not remove all confounding.

Weekly retrospective reports are subject to recall and interpretation differences. Four check-ins are a sparse repeated-measures design: they provide limited within-person information, make relationship-specific features unstable, and leave missingness and attrition potentially informative. The gap is undefined for someone who never reports wanting to share. The system must represent these cases as missing or insufficient rather than as zero.

The APA SAD-D Adult severity measure is not a diagnosis. Although adult clinical and community studies support its psychometric properties and the official instructions support repeated use, evidence is not specific to a four-week U.S. convenience sample ages 18–22. The measure may provide more information at higher symptom severity and may have limited discrimination at the low end. Permission to reproduce an instrument and scientific suitability remain separate questions.

A convenience sample will not represent all people ages 18–22. Small samples limit precision, model complexity, subgroup analysis, and transportability. Subgroup results must be suppressed when disclosure risk is material. Ages 14–17 are outside scope and would require a separate protocol and protections.

Predictors about comfort and judgment overlap conceptually with social-anxiety items. A behavioral primary feature, separate outcome form, frozen optional protocol, and leakage sensitivity analysis reduce but do not eliminate circularity. Null, negative, or uninformative results must be reported plainly, without reframing exploratory patterns as confirmation.
